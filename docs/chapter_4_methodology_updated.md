# Chapter 4: METHODOLOGY

Chapter 4 outlines the research methods and data strategies used to fulfill the study’s objectives. This chapter is critical for demonstrating the reliability and validity of the approach. It focuses on identifying where data originates and how it is collected, processed, and measured—organized clearly per specific research objective to ensure methodological alignment throughout the study.

## Sources of Data

The Sources of Data section details where and from whom the empirical and technical information is obtained in order to fulfill each specific objective of the study. This encompasses primary sources, including structured surveys, key informant interviews, direct user observations, automated system logs, and code execution telemetry, as well as secondary sources, such as institutional policies, academic literature, standardized evaluation frameworks, and departmental archival records. For each specific objective, the corresponding data source directly substantiates the required analysis and system functionality.

**Research objective 1** employed both primary and secondary data sources to establish the functional, non-functional, and workflow requirements of the proposed system. Primary data were gathered through semi-structured key informant interviews (KIIs) and consultative focus group discussions (FGDs) conducted with academic stakeholders directly engaged in the defense governance lifecycle at Partido State University. These respondents comprised Department Research Coordinators, College Deans, Department Chairs, Thesis/Capstone Advisers, Panel Chairs, and graduating 4th-year student researchers across the five (5) academic colleges: the College of Engineering and Computational Sciences (CECS), the College of Business and Management (CBM), the College of Education (COE), the College of Science (COS), and the College of Arts and Humanities (CAH). Purposive sampling was utilized to recruit informants with direct operational familiarity with defense administration. Secondary data sources included Commission on Higher Education (CHED) Memorandum Orders (CMO No. 25, Series of 2015 for Information Technology Education and CMO No. 15, Series of 2019 for Graduate Programs), the official Partido State University Research and Capstone Manual, institutional academic calendars, and archival Department of Computational Sciences (DCS) defense routing documents, specifically forms DCS-CF-01 through DCS-CF-05.

**Research objective 2** utilized comparative design benchmarking, structural software engineering models, and expert mentorship review to formulate the system architecture, database schema, user interface (UI), and multi-role workflows of AURORA. Primary data sources included iterative consultations, critique feedback, and formal architectural verification by the Thesis/Capstone Adviser and Department of Computational Sciences (DCS) faculty, who critically evaluated the models and data schemas against institutional defense standards. Additionally, primary data encompassed user-centered wireframe critiques and usability feedback from prospective student and faculty users. Secondary data sources were gathered through systematic comparative benchmarking of contemporary educational workflow systems, academic defense portals, and modern web application UI design systems to identify intuitive visual hierarchies, aesthetic color combinations, and ergonomic dashboard patterns. These secondary sources were integrated with formal software engineering standards, including Entity-Relationship modeling for normalized relational databases and Unified Modeling Language (UML) specifications for architectural blueprints.

**Research objective 3** relied on programmatic, system-level execution telemetry, automated software testing outputs, and database runtime metrics to drive the development, integration, and verification of the AURORA defense workflow engine. Primary data sources included TypeScript compiler diagnostics, automated test coverage reports generated via Jest/Vitest unit test harnesses, Playwright end-to-end (E2E) browser simulation logs, Next.js production build bundle analyzers, and Supabase real-time replication audit logs. These automated sources provided continuous telemetry on component render performance, PDF coordinate annotation rendering latency, and database query execution times under simulated concurrent load. Secondary data sources included official technical documentation for Next.js 15 App Router, Supabase PostgreSQL SDKs, Tailwind CSS styling tokens, and Git version-controlled commit histories tracking code stability across iterative sprint releases.

**Research objective 4** utilized comprehensive empirical data collected through a structured evaluation survey administered to a total of $N = 101$ academic stakeholders following their active, hands-on interaction with the deployed AURORA system. The participant sample was comprised of ninety-eight (98) graduating fourth-year students (97.0%) actively completing their undergraduate capstone and thesis defenses, one (1) Academic Research Coordinator (1.0%), one (1) Thesis/Capstone Adviser and Faculty Panelist (1.0%), and one (1) University Academic Leader / College Dean (1.0%). The sample demonstrated robust institutional representation across all five (5) academic colleges of Partido State University: the College of Business & Management ($n = 28$, 27.7%), the College of Engineering and Computational Sciences ($n = 26$, 25.7%), the College of Education ($n = 17$, 16.8%), the College of Science ($n = 17$, 16.8%), and the College of Arts and Humanities ($n = 13$, 12.9%). Demographically, the sample was 61.4% female ($n = 62$) and 38.6% male ($n = 39$), with 61.4% aged 20–22 years ($n = 62$), 19.8% aged 17–19 years ($n = 20$), 17.8% aged 23–29 years ($n = 18$), and 1.0% aged 30 years or older ($n = 1$). Stratified quota sampling combined with purposive sampling was employed to ensure proportional representation across diverse disciplines, including technical computing programs, business disciplines, teacher education, natural sciences, and humanities. Secondary data sources included the normative psychometric benchmarks of the System Usability Scale (SUS) established by Brooke (1996) and Bangor, Kortum, and Miller (2008), the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) model developed by Venkatesh et al. (2003), and the ISO/IEC 25010:2011 Software Product Quality Model.

## Data Instrumentation

The data instrumentation process includes the development, adaptation, and validation of instruments that were designed to methodically gather, generate, and analyze data associated with the four research objectives of AURORA. Standardized instruments were adopted where available to ensure empirical comparability, while researcher-developed instruments underwent rigorous content validation and verification prior to full administration.

**For Research Objective 1**, this study used a semi-structured interview approach to gather detailed insights from Students, Advisers, Defense Panelists, and Research Coordinators about their existing workflows, difficulties faced in the manual procedure, and expectations for automation. The interview guide comprised open-ended enquiries aimed at investigating workflow difficulties, issues with the current paper-based system such as manual routing delays in departmental clearance forms DCS-CF-01 through DCS-CF-05, anticipations for a digital solution, and recommendations for system attributes and capabilities. This was supplemented by an Institutional Document Analysis Checklist that systematically reviewed historical defense evaluation forms, marking criteria, and grading transmittal records to identify essential data entities and governance rules for digitization. All qualitative instruments were pre-validated by the Department of Computational Sciences Capstone Committee to verify alignment with institutional defense policies.

**For Research Objective 2**, the study employed standard software engineering design instruments, visual workflow modeling tools, comparative UI/UX benchmarking rubrics, and an adviser review protocol to develop and validate the system’s architectural blueprint. To establish relational integrity, Entity-Relationship Diagrams (ERDs) adhering to Crow’s Foot notation were utilized to model database entities, attributes, primary and foreign keys, and cardinalities, ensuring third normal form and Boyce-Codd Normal Form compliance to prevent update and deletion anomalies. Role-based functional boundaries and user permissions were charted using Unified Modeling Language (UML) Use Case Diagrams, while cross-functional defense lifecycles were delineated through Swimlane Activity Diagrams. These swimlanes mapped sequential handoffs across student submissions, adviser endorsements, live panel deliberations, and coordinator verdict confirmations. In parallel, a comparative UI/UX benchmarking rubric was deployed to inspect existing educational workflow platforms, identifying ergonomic interface hierarchies, clear typography, and aesthetically pleasing color palettes with accessible contrast ratios. All resulting diagrams, data models, and interface wireframes were evaluated and formally approved by the Capstone Adviser through an architectural review protocol before system development commenced.

**For Research Objective 3**, the study deployed a comprehensive Software Verification and Instrumentation Suite comprising automated test harnesses, static code analyzers, and database performance profilers. Static typing integrity was enforced and verified through the TypeScript 5 Strict Compiler, guaranteeing zero implicit type coercions across server-client boundaries. Functional correctness was validated using Vitest and Jest automated unit test suites covering rubric grading algorithms, grade weighting formulas, and panel deliberation consensus logic. End-to-end workflow transitions were measured and validated using Playwright automated browser test scripts that simulated multi-user concurrent defense sessions. Database query performance and index optimization were measured using PostgreSQL EXPLAIN ANALYZE telemetry, recording query latency, disk cache hit ratios, and Row-Level Security evaluation times to verify that database transaction latency remained below 100 milliseconds under load.

**For Research Objective 4**, the study administered a dual-model, standardized empirical survey instrument that rigorously combined the System Usability Scale (SUS) by Brooke (1996) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) model by Venkatesh et al. (2003), aligned with ISO/IEC 25010 software product quality criteria. The instrument comprised thirty-seven closed-ended rating items structured across demographic profiling, usability assessment, and technology acceptance. The demographic profile gathered institutional metadata, role, college affiliation, year level, age, and gender across nine items. The System Usability Scale utilized ten items in an alternating format of positive and negative statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Individual responses were converted using Brooke’s standard algorithm where odd-numbered items were scored by subtracting one and even-numbered items by subtracting the response from five, with the cumulative sum multiplied by 2.5 to generate a composite score ranging from 0 to 100. The UTAUT battery evaluated 27 items across six core constructs on a 5-point Likert scale: Performance Expectancy with three items measuring perceived utility and task efficiency; Effort Expectancy with four items assessing ease of use and learnability; Social Influence with four items evaluating peer, mentor, and administrative endorsement; Facilitating Conditions with four items examining technical resources and organizational support; Behavioral Intention with three items capturing intent and future adoption plans; and Use Behavior with nine items quantifying experiential satisfaction, paperless workflow preference, and recommendation propensity.

Prior to deployment, the survey instrument was subjected to formal content and face validation by a panel of three IT education and software engineering experts using the Good and Scates (1972) criteria across six dimensions: clarity of directions, relevance to objectives, vocabulary appropriateness, construct alignment, objectivity, and suitability to respondents. The expert panel awarded a composite grand mean of 4.89 out of 5.00, verbally interpreted as "Very Highly Valid". During empirical administration to 101 university stakeholders across all five academic colleges, the 37-item instrument achieved an overall Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.817, demonstrating high internal consistency. The 27-item UTAUT construct battery demonstrated strong reliability with an alpha of 0.862, with individual construct reliabilities of 0.724 for Performance Expectancy, 0.742 for Effort Expectancy, 0.664 for Social Influence, 0.627 for Facilitating Conditions, 0.690 for Behavioral Intention, and 0.718 for Use Behavior. The 10-item System Usability Scale showed acceptable reliability with a Cronbach's Alpha of 0.695. The evaluation produced an overall mean SUS score of 70.84 with a standard deviation of 14.17, surpassing the international benchmark of 68.0 and earning a "Good / Acceptable" adjective rating and Grade B classification. The composite UTAUT grand mean reached 4.44 with a standard deviation of 0.27, representing "Strongly Agree / Very High Acceptance" across all individual constructs, specifically Performance Expectancy at 4.55, Effort Expectancy at 4.40, Social Influence at 4.41, Facilitating Conditions at 4.44, Behavioral Intention at 4.45, and Use Behavior at 4.41.

Structuring this chapter per objective ensures methodological clarity and consistency. For each objective, readers should easily see: (1) what data is needed, (2) where it will come from, and (3) how it will be collected or measured. This alignment strengthens the credibility of your research design and makes it easier to evaluate the effectiveness of your methodology in achieving your goals.
